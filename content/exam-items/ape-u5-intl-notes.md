## 1. Trade theories

| Theory | Author | Basis of trade |
|---|---|---|
| Mercantilism | Thomas Mun and others | export surplus = gold = wealth |
| **Absolute advantage** | Adam Smith (1776) | lower absolute cost |
| **Comparative advantage** | David Ricardo (1817) | lower opportunity cost; labour theory of value |
| Reciprocal demand / offer curves | J.S. Mill, Marshall, Edgeworth | demand decides terms of trade |
| Opportunity cost | Haberler (1936) | PPF replaces labour theory |
| **Factor endowment (H–O)** | Heckscher (1919), Ohlin (1933) | a country exports goods that use its **abundant** factor intensively |
| Specific factors | Viner, Jones | short-run factor immobility |
| Technology gap | Posner (1961) | innovation lead |
| **Product life cycle** | Raymond Vernon (1966) | new → mature → standardised; production moves to LDCs |
| Similar preferences / overlapping demand | Linder (1961) | manufactures traded between countries with similar incomes |
| **New trade theory** | Krugman (1979–80) | economies of scale, product variety → intra-industry trade |
| New new trade theory | Melitz (2003) | firm heterogeneity: only productive firms export |
| Gravity model | Tinbergen (1962) | trade ∝ size, inversely ∝ distance |

### Theorems from the H–O model
- **Factor price equalisation** (Samuelson, 1948): free trade equalises factor prices across countries.
- **Stolper–Samuelson** (1941): a rise in the price of a good raises the real return of the factor used intensively in it and lowers the other's.
- **Rybczynski** (1955): at constant prices, an increase in one factor raises output of the good using it intensively and **reduces** output of the other good.
- **Leontief paradox** (1953): US, capital-abundant, exported labour-intensive goods. Explanations: human capital, natural resources, factor intensity reversal, demand bias, trade barriers.

## 2. Terms of trade (TOT)
| Concept | Formula |
|---|---|
| Net barter (commodity) TOT | Px / Pm × 100 (Taussig) |
| Gross barter TOT | Qm / Qx (Taussig) |
| Income TOT | (Px / Pm)·Qx (Dorrance): capacity to import |
| Single factoral TOT | (Px / Pm)·Zx (productivity in exports) |
| Double factoral TOT | (Px / Pm)·(Zx / Zm) |

- **Prebisch–Singer thesis** (1950): primary-goods exporters face a secular decline in TOT.
- **Immiserising growth** (Bhagwati, 1958): export-biased growth can make a country worse off if TOT fall sharply.

## 3. Trade policy

![Tariff welfare effects](uploads/diagrams/tariff.svg)

- Small country tariff: consumer surplus falls; producer surplus and government revenue rise; **net loss = production distortion (b) + consumption distortion (d)**.
- Large country can gain by improving its TOT: **optimum tariff** t* = 1/(e* − 1), where e* = elasticity of foreign offer curve.
- **Metzler paradox**: a tariff may lower the domestic price of the imported good.
- **Effective rate of protection** (Corden, Johnson): ERP = (t − a·tᵢ)/(1 − a), where a = share of imported inputs, tᵢ = tariff on inputs. Tariff escalation raises ERP on finished goods.
- Quota: like a tariff, but revenue goes to licence holders (quota rent); an import quota under monopoly is worse than an equivalent tariff.
- **Dumping**: selling abroad below home price (international price discrimination). Anti-dumping and countervailing duties.
- Arguments for protection: **infant industry** (Hamilton, List), optimum tariff, terms of trade, employment, national security.
- **Customs union theory** (Viner, 1950): **trade creation** (good) vs **trade diversion** (bad).
- Integration levels: preferential trade area → free trade area → customs union → common market → economic union (Balassa).

## 4. Balance of payments (BoP)
- **Current account**: trade in goods (visible), services (invisibles), primary income, secondary income (remittances).
- **Capital/financial account**: FDI, FPI, loans, banking capital, change in reserves.
- BoP always balances in accounting; "deficit" means the autonomous transactions do not balance.
- **Adjustment approaches**:
  - **Elasticity approach**: **Marshall–Lerner condition**: devaluation improves trade balance if |ex| + |em| > 1. **J-curve**: trade balance first worsens, then improves.
  - **Absorption approach** (Alexander, 1952): B = Y − A; devaluation works only if output rises or absorption falls.
  - **Monetary approach** (Johnson, Mundell): BoP deficit = excess money supply.
- **Swan diagram**: internal and external balance need two instruments (expenditure changing + expenditure switching). **Tinbergen rule**: as many instruments as targets. **Mundell's assignment**: monetary policy to external balance, fiscal to internal (under fixed rates).

## 5. Exchange rates
- **Purchasing power parity** (Cassel, 1916): absolute (E = P/P*) and relative (%ΔE = π − π*).
- **Interest rate parity**: covered and uncovered.
- **Overshooting** (Dornbusch, 1976): with sticky prices, the exchange rate overshoots its long-run level after a monetary shock.
- Regimes: fixed (gold standard, Bretton Woods 1944–71), floating, managed float (India), crawling peg, currency board.
- **Impossible trinity** (Mundell–Fleming): a country cannot have fixed exchange rate, free capital movement and independent monetary policy at the same time.
- **Optimum currency area**: Mundell (1961), McKinnon, Kenen.
- **Triffin dilemma**: a reserve-currency country must run deficits, which erodes confidence.
- **Balassa–Samuelson effect**: richer countries have higher price levels because of productivity in tradables.

## Exam traps
- Comparative, not absolute, advantage is enough for gainful trade.
- Rybczynski holds commodity prices **constant**; Stolper–Samuelson changes them.
- Marshall–Lerner: sum of elasticities > 1 (in absolute value), starting from balanced trade.
- Under the impossible trinity, India chooses a managed exchange rate with partial capital controls and independent monetary policy.
- Trade diversion moves imports from a low-cost outsider to a higher-cost member.
