## Part A: Environmental economics

### 1. Environment as an economic problem
- Environment gives resources, absorbs waste, provides amenities. Many environmental goods are **public goods** or **common-pool resources**, so markets fail.
- **Tragedy of the commons** (Garrett Hardin, 1968). **Elinor Ostrom** (Nobel 2009) showed communities can manage commons through local institutions.
- **Materials balance** approach (Kneese, Ayres, d'Arge).

### 2. Correcting externalities
| Tool | Idea |
|---|---|
| **Pigouvian tax** (A.C. Pigou, *Economics of Welfare*, 1920) | tax = marginal external damage at efficient output |
| **Coase bargaining** (1960) | clear property rights + low transaction costs → efficient result |
| Command and control | emission standards |
| **Tradable permits** (cap and trade; J.H. Dales 1968, Montgomery) | fixed total pollution, market sets price; cost-effective |
| Polluter pays principle | OECD (1972) |
| Baumol–Oates standards and charges | reach a standard at least cost |

- **Weitzman (1974)**: prices vs quantities: when marginal damage is steep, quantity controls are better; when marginal abatement cost is steep, taxes are better.

### 3. Valuing the environment
- **Total economic value** = use value (direct, indirect, option) + non-use value (existence, bequest).
| Method | Type | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Contingent valuation** | stated preference (WTP/WTA survey) | value of a clean river |
| Choice experiments | stated preference | |
| **Travel cost method** | revealed preference | value of a national park |
| **Hedonic pricing** | revealed preference | house prices near polluted areas |
| Averting / defensive expenditure | revealed | water purifiers |
| Dose–response, replacement cost | production function | crop loss from pollution |

### 4. Sustainable development and natural resources
- **Brundtland Report** (*Our Common Future*, 1987): development that meets present needs without compromising future generations' ability to meet theirs.
- **Weak sustainability** (Hartwick–Solow: total capital non-declining; natural capital can be substituted) vs **strong sustainability** (critical natural capital must be preserved).
- **Hartwick rule**: invest the rents from exhaustible resources in reproducible capital.
- **Hotelling rule** (1931): the net price (rent) of an exhaustible resource rises at the rate of interest.
- Renewable resources: maximum sustainable yield (fisheries, Gordon–Schaefer model); open access leads to over-harvesting.
- **Green GDP / SEEA** (System of Environmental-Economic Accounting).
- *Limits to Growth* (Club of Rome, Meadows, 1972).

### 5. Environmental Kuznets curve
Pollution first rises and then falls as per-capita income grows (Grossman & Krueger, 1991). Evidence is mixed; holds better for local pollutants (SO₂) than for CO₂.

### 6. Climate change: agreements
| Event | Year | Key outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Stockholm Conference | 1972 | UNEP created |
| Montreal Protocol | 1987 | ozone-depleting substances; **Kigali Amendment 2016** (HFCs) |
| Rio Earth Summit | 1992 | **UNFCCC**, CBD, Agenda 21 |
| **Kyoto Protocol** | 1997 (in force 2005) | binding targets for developed countries; CDM, JI, emissions trading |
| **Paris Agreement** (COP21) | 2015 | keep warming well below 2°C, aim 1.5°C; **NDCs** |
| COP26 Glasgow | 2021 | India: **net zero by 2070**, "Panchamrit" |
| COP28 Dubai | 2023 | first global stocktake; loss and damage fund operational |
| COP29 Baku | 2024 | new climate finance goal (NCQG): $300 bn/yr by 2035 |
| COP30 Belém (Brazil) | 2025 | |

- Principle: **common but differentiated responsibilities (CBDR)**.
- India's updated NDC (2022): reduce emissions intensity of GDP by 45% from 2005 level by 2030; 50% of installed power capacity from non-fossil sources by 2030.
- **Stern Review** (2006): cost of inaction on climate > cost of action. **Nordhaus** (DICE model; Nobel 2018 with Romer).

## Part B: Demography

### 7. Population theories
| Theory | Author | Claim |
|---|---|---|
| **Malthusian theory** (1798) | T.R. Malthus | population grows geometrically, food arithmetically; positive and preventive checks |
| Optimum population | Cannan, Robbins, Dalton | population that maximises per-capita income |
| **Demographic transition** | Notestein, Thompson | high birth and death → falling death → falling birth → low both |
| Biological theories | Sadler, Doubleday | |
| Boserup (1965) | Ester Boserup | population pressure drives agricultural innovation |
| Becker | Gary Becker | children as durable goods; quantity–quality trade-off |

![Demographic transition](uploads/diagrams/demographic-transition.svg)

### 8. Key measures
- **Crude birth rate / death rate**: per 1000 population.
- **Total fertility rate (TFR)**: average children per woman over her lifetime. **Replacement level = 2.1**.
- **Net reproduction rate (NRR)** = 1 at replacement.
- Infant mortality rate (IMR): deaths under age 1 per 1000 live births. **MMR**: maternal deaths per 1 lakh live births.
- **Dependency ratio** = (population 0–14 + 65+) / population 15–64.
- Sex ratio (India: females per 1000 males). Child sex ratio (0–6).
- **Demographic dividend**: growth boost when the working-age share rises.

## Exam traps
- Coase theorem needs low transaction costs; with high costs, the allocation of rights matters.
- Hotelling: resource **rent** rises at the rate of interest (not the price including extraction cost).
- Travel cost and hedonic pricing are revealed-preference; contingent valuation is stated-preference.
- TFR of 2.1 is replacement level; NRR of 1 is replacement level.
- India's net-zero year is 2070, not 2050.
